Everyone is betting on how AI reshapes the world, whether they've noticed or not. What to learn, what to build, what to buy, where to work. Those are all wagers on what the next decade looks like, and they get placed either way — the only choice is whether you place them deliberately. Most people never write their bets down, so they never see how their own assumptions are driving their decisions, or how prepared they actually are for what's coming.

These are mine, written down. Seven predictions about where AI takes software, work, and the physical world, each with the reasoning behind it and why it matters for how we act now. I'm sharing them because they shape how I spend my time: the projects I take on, what I study and read, the people I surround myself with. If this is where we're headed, I want to shape it into the best possible version for all people. I consider myself an optimist, but I grew up organizing, in nonprofits and progressive campaigns, and that optimism is the kind of hope you have to work hard as hell for. Each of these bets could go terribly wrong or beautifully well, and it's up to each of us.

Each bet will get its own full post. The first, on Bet A, is already up: [The Living Product](/posts/the-living-product).

## What I'm treating as *settled*

Before the bets, six things I consider ground truth. Not everyone accepts them yet, but the evidence is in.

1. **90%+ of code will soon be written by AI.** Anthropic reports over 80% of code merged into its production codebase now comes from Claude. Google reports three quarters. The slope isn't in dispute.
2. **Writing code is almost free. Knowing what to build isn't.** Judgment about what a signal means and which problem is worth solving didn't get cheaper. That's now most of the job.
3. **We know how to build most of what people need. We can't get it built.** Clean water, reliable power, and housing are solved on paper. Permitting, procurement, and liability are what stop them. Nuclear is the clearest case: most of its cost blowup came from paperwork and supervision, not concrete and steel. It's the friction of the real world that stands in our way, and to date, technology has had a limited ability to resolve it.
4. **Optimization never finishes.** Every model update resets part of the tuning. And every workflow can still be made faster, cheaper, or more accurate than it currently is. This work grows rather than shrinks as models improve, because better models mean more agents doing more things. Becoming an AI-driven company is a commitment to a process, not a migration.
5. **Energy is the ceiling on everything.** Push far enough on water, food, materials, or compute and each one turns into an energy problem. Jensen Huang puts energy at the base of the AI stack and calls it the binding constraint on how much intelligence can be produced — the man selling chips telling you chips aren't the limit.
6. **Bad software dies of old age, not competition.** PowerSchool has been beaten on product for a decade and it's fine. Banks still run COBOL. What keeps these systems alive is contracts, certifications, and a buyer who will lose their job if the migration fails.

<BetHero
  letter="A"
  src="/posts/seven-bets/bet-a.webp"
  alt="A workbench of instruments and glassware overgrown by a greenhouse, plants and machinery grown into one another."
  caption="A loop, not an object — sense, interpret, grow, prune."
>Software will be grown, not *built*</BetHero>

For all of software history, humans decided what should exist and then constructed it by hand. Every feature debated, specified, designed, and attached. And once shipped, the product just sits there — when the world changes, when users change, when a policy shifts, it does nothing. It waits for humans to notice and rebuild.

AI has so far mostly accelerated that old model: the code gets written faster, but the product is still a static object humans maintain from the outside.

The bet is that software stops being an object and starts running a loop. It senses its environment: how users actually behave, what support keeps hearing, what changed in the market or the rules. It interprets what's signal and what's noise. It grows responses, tests them, keeps what works, and prunes what stopped earning its place. Its form emerges from its environment the way a plant's does — but only inside a garden humans design.

We write the rules for what it's allowed to become, choose which signals it trusts, and define the purpose it grows toward. The human job shifts from specifying features to specifying the conditions under which features emerge.

<WhyItMatters lead="The garden is a real stack of hard problems, and nobody has it production-ready.">

First, for what you should be learning. Gathering and routing the signals a product should see. Building the judgment layer that separates signal from noise. Generating the evals and verification that keep growth pointed at the goal. The people who learn to build it are learning the scarcest skill in software.

Second, for what happens if nobody does. A loop has to optimize something, and if no one chooses carefully, it optimizes whatever was easy to measure: engagement, clicks, sales. We ran that experiment once. Social media was a single adaptive loop, optimizing a single lazy metric, and it was enough to bend politics, attention, and a generation's mental health. This bet says every product gets a loop. Same failure, at 10 to 100 times the surface area.

</WhyItMatters>

<BetHero
  letter="B"
  src="/posts/seven-bets/bet-b.webp"
  alt="Many framed windows suspended on cords above a single workbench, each opening onto a different landscape."
  caption="Payroll as an investment portfolio — many bets, real stakes."
>Everyone works like an entrepreneur, and employment starts carrying *equity*</BetHero>

Agents take everything with a knowable answer — in Cynefin's terms, the simple and the complicated work, where the right response can be worked out in advance. What's left is the work where nobody can tell you the target: reading a situation nobody has read before, deciding how to respond, setting the boundaries the machines run inside.

There is nearly unlimited work of that kind in water, energy, food, education, and housing. Almost none of it is funded, because figuring out how to make it pay *is* the work. So companies start treating payroll like an investment portfolio.

And since a salary caps the upside while an unlock doesn't, employment has to carry equity, or the people capable of the biggest unlocks keep leaving to found them.

<WhyItMatters lead="The bargain attached to specifiable work disappears with the work, and nothing replaces it unless it's built.">

If you're employed, start reading a salary-only offer as a signal — it says the company expects your work to be specifiable in advance, and specifiable work is exactly what's going to agents. Ask what you'd own if you found something big.

If you run a company, build the portfolio deliberately: spread bets across your people and let them hold a real stake in what they unlock, because the alternative is watching your best people leave to own it themselves.

</WhyItMatters>

<BetHero
  letter="C"
  src="/posts/seven-bets/bet-c.webp"
  alt="Dozens of brass instruments hung on wires in orbital arcs above a bench of machines, all running at once."
  caption="The leverage question: how many systems can you hold at once."
>Managing people gives way to managing *machines*</BetHero>

The Cynefin framework splits work into four domains: simple and complicated, where the answer is knowable if you have the expertise, and complex and chaotic, where it isn't — where you probe, read the response, and adjust.

AI takes the first two completely in the digital world, and eventually in the physical one (see Bet E for why *eventually* is doing heavy lifting). What's left for people is the other half: living in the complex and the chaotic, while supervising the machines that run the simple and the complicated.

The supervision is real, ongoing work, and it scales in a particular way: with how many distinct systems you run, not how many agents run inside them. A few people can hold what used to be a department.

Robots are different — a machine in a room breaks in its own particular way, so that work scales with headcount. Physical labor stays labor-shaped. Digital work doesn't.

<WhyItMatters lead="Living in the complex while managing the complicated has always been the leadership playbook. The bet is that it becomes everyone's.">

Almost nobody has been trained for it. Our education and training systems mostly produce complicated-domain workers, and that's the domain that's leaving.

So the career skill to build is running systems: the leverage question stops being *how many people report to you* and becomes *how many distinct workflows can you hold at once*. Start counting your systems.

</WhyItMatters>

<BetHero
  letter="D"
  src="/posts/seven-bets/bet-d.webp"
  alt="A great cross-section of tree rings beside smaller ones, decades of growth held in the grain."
  caption="Capabilities get matched. Accumulated state does not."
>Most software becomes worthless. Value settles in *memory*.</BetHero>

Most software is about to be disposable: generated for one decision, used once, deleted, worth nothing the day after. The bet is about the exception. What persists is what accumulates.

Compute and reasoning are where the money gets spent. Memory is where it stays. Capabilities get matched within months: open weights caught GPT-4, and a hedge fund built DeepSeek.

What can't be copied is accumulated state, meaning whatever a system has learned about one particular user, market, or problem. Difficulty is a cost. Accumulation is a moat.

<WhyItMatters lead="Who owns the accumulating layer is being decided now, in contracts.">

Stop paying for software and start paying for what software holds. If you buy it, ask every vendor what the system will contain after two years that couldn't be rebuilt in an afternoon — and who owns that, whether you can export it, whether the learning flows back to you or only to them.

If you build it, know that a thin layer over a frontier model is a countdown clock; the vendor ships your feature natively next quarter.

</WhyItMatters>

<BetHero
  letter="E"
  src="/posts/seven-bets/bet-e.webp"
  alt="A vast construction site of scaffolding, cranes and half-built towers, with small bright screens floating in the dark above it."
  caption="Believe the software half. Add a decade to the atoms half."
>Digital speed tells you nothing about *physical* speed</BetHero>

Software got fast because iteration is free. You can try a thousand things before lunch and throw the failures away. That engine stops the moment a problem touches atoms.

Robotics trials wear out hardware. A materials candidate has to survive a real furnace. Every self-driving mile is an actual mile, which is why Waymo spent fifteen years on a problem nobody was blocking.

The more physical a problem is, the less AI helps. And the problems that determine whether people live decently are the most physical ones we have.

<WhyItMatters lead="Every abundance timeline you read needs to be split in two. Believe the software half. Add a decade to the atoms half.">

Any plan, investment, or career move that depends on physical infrastructure arriving on a software schedule is mispriced.

And notice what the split means: the physical problems will not be oversubscribed. If you want work where showing up actually changes the outcome, it's there, it runs on decade timelines, and the honest number said out loud at the start is what separates the projects that finish from the ones pitched on software speed.

</WhyItMatters>

<BetHero
  letter="F"
  src="/posts/seven-bets/bet-f.webp"
  alt="A valley of energy infrastructure at sunrise — a huge dish array, wind turbines along the ridges, and a working plant beside the water."
  caption="Generation got cheap. Delivery is where the cost went."
>Energy is the defining *buildout* of the decade</BetHero>

For most of living memory, electricity was a background utility. From 2010 to 2023, global demand grew about 2.7% a year, and in the US it barely grew at all for fifteen years. That era just ended.

Demand growth nearly doubled in 2024, and the IEA now projects 40 to 50% more demand by 2035, roughly twice the pace of the last decade stacked on top of it. The drivers compound: a hotter world buying air conditioners, rising incomes across developing economies buying appliances and building industry, and AI. Cooling alone adds a Japan and Korea's worth of demand by 2035.

The money has already moved, with clean energy drawing $2.2 trillion this year, nearly double what's going to fossil fuels. So have the jobs: the two fastest-growing occupations in the entire US economy are wind turbine technician and solar installer, and the real need behind them is millions of construction, grid, and trades jobs the training pipeline isn't producing.

Generation is the part that got cheap. Solar and battery costs fell around 80% in a decade. But bills never fell, and they're now rising faster than inflation, because the cost moved to delivery: wires, substations, storm hardening, aging equipment replaced at above-inflation prices. Delivery is now where utilities spend the most, and delivery is atoms, so the last bet says it doesn't speed up.

So watch what those with money are already doing: bypassing the grid entirely. Data centers build on-site gas turbines because the interconnection queue is slower than constructing a power plant. Wealthy households exit with rooftop solar and batteries. Every defection concentrates the grid's fixed costs on whoever remains, and behind-the-meter buildout escapes most of the governance the grid carries, meaning the clean standards, the shared costs, the obligation to serve. Left alone, the buildout is fast, gassy, and private.

<WhyItMatters lead="This is several of the decade's biggest problems in one place, and none of them solve themselves.">

It's an innovation problem: distribution and storage are where costs didn't collapse, and delivery is the open technical frontier. It's a workforce problem: the fastest-growing work in the country needs a training pipeline that doesn't exist yet.

And it's a regulation problem twice over: whether what gets built is clean when gas is the fast path, and who pays for the shared grid as the wealthy exit it. The clean question and the fairness question turn out to be the same question. Does the buildout happen inside a system with rules, or around it?

</WhyItMatters>

<BetHero
  letter="G"
  src="/posts/seven-bets/bet-g.webp"
  alt="A long banquet table heavy with fruit and candlelight, raised on a platform above a small boat working the dark water below."
  caption="Free things travel. Grids, hospitals, and roads do not."
>Abundance arrives for the wealthy first, and the bill lands on *everyone else*</BetHero>

Equitable distribution has never happened on its own. HIV drugs cost about $14,000 a year in 1990. They cost under $100 a year in sub-Saharan Africa today. None of that was automatic. Activists, generic manufacturers in India, and a few governments broke the pricing structure on purpose, and it took them twenty years.

AI abundance follows the same pattern. Intelligence will be nearly free, and free things travel — everyone gets smarter. What doesn't travel is the power grid, the hospital, and the road, because those get built in place and gated by permits and capital.

So the gap in what people know closes while the gap in what they can build widens, and the energy and emissions behind the top decile's abundance land hardest on the people least able to absorb them.

The bet is that the forcing doesn't happen this time. The effort going into correcting this distribution is nowhere near the size of the thing it's meant to correct, and there's no sign of the twenty-year push the HIV case required. The people promising a world where money and jobs stop mattering already live in one. They're describing their own lives and calling it everyone's future.

<WhyItMatters lead="The work that matters most is building productive capacity in the places the default path skips.">

Cold storage for farmers who lose a third of what they grow. Local power for towns the grid isn't scheduled to reach. Water connections. Not aid, not devices: capacity.

And it starts now, because the forcing takes twenty years and the clock is already running. This is the one bet I want to lose.

</WhyItMatters>
