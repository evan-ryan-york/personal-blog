<Lead>
Meet Denise. She's an accountant at a Cleveland company that makes dialysis machines. She's thirty years into her career. Her team closes the books on time, and if a number looks wrong, she's usually the one who catches it. One Monday, her company turns on an AI system that can handle much of that work.
</Lead>

<Turn>What happens to Denise next is the problem I can't stop thinking about.</Turn>

In one version of the next three years, Denise's company cuts staff as AI takes on more of the work. She loses her job, along with millions of people like her. Work that took a hundred people takes ten. Margins grow and shareholders celebrate. All the while, kids keep dying of preventable diseases, billions of people still lack safe drinking water, and housing stays out of reach. We get very efficient at the work we already know how to do. The problems we haven't figured out are still there.

In the other version, the company uses some of the savings to give Denise a new job. For years, clinic directors have told the sales team, "We'd buy another machine if we could afford it." Denise's job is to figure out what it would take to sell to those clinics so they can treat more patients.

She takes a parts list to the engineers and asks, "If we had to sell this machine for half the price, what would we have to do differently?"

Her manager gives her a budget and time in her working week to try things. They meet to talk through what she's learning and decide what's worth testing next. If she helps open a new market, she gets a share of what she helped build.

AI gets the books closed in both futures. The bet I want companies to make is that people like Denise can learn to solve problems those companies have been stuck on for years. Put millions of people to work that way, and we could grow businesses by making things people already need, from dialysis to safe drinking water, affordable to more people.

<Chapter number="Act I">The work AI takes on first</Chapter>

It took Denise years to get this good at closing the books. She can teach another accountant how to do it and tell whether they got it right. Over the next several years, almost every task we do on a computer that passes that test is going to AI.

Making dialysis machines affordable for more rural clinics is a different kind of problem. Denise might suspect one expensive part is driving up the cost. She might look for grants. She has to find people who can help test those ideas and decide which ones are worth another week. AI can help throughout. When the results surprise them, Denise and her team have to decide what to try next.

The Cynefin framework gives us language for that distinction.

<CynefinPlate caption="Four domains, and how much of each a machine can take." />

AI takes on tasks and repeatable workflows. What happens to the job depends on what else needs doing and what the company does with the time it gets back. A job can include work you can teach and check alongside decisions about what to do in the first place.

Look at software development. A developer can ask AI to write code once she can describe what it should do and test whether it works. She can spend some of the time she gets back watching a customer get stuck, figuring out why, and trying two different fixes with them. Her team can also take on projects it used to leave on the backlog.

That's complex work. You have to figure out what to do when the answer is still open. A company wants to enter a new market. A school needs its students to learn more math. A hospital keeps trying to figure out why patients fall through the gaps.

Picture Denise walking into a clinic that could finally afford a machine she helped make cheaper. Her company has a new customer. Someone who used to spend half the day traveling for treatment can get it closer to home. Back at her desk the next morning, Denise knows who she's doing it for.

<PostImage src="/posts/when-ai-does-our-work/drought-tower.webp" alt="A Renaissance-style fresco of a drought-stricken hill town seen from above: dead trees cast long shadows over cracked, sun-baked earth, while a tall stone tower rises from the dry ground with a lush rooftop garden, an orange tree and a pool of water on top, and below, a crowd with clay jugs gathers around a wooden pump at its base." />

Give more people that chance and companies can take on problems they've wanted to solve for years. Some of those attempts will pay off for the business and its customers. More people like Denise get to spend their working lives solving problems they care about.

<Chapter number="Act II">What if we don't need less human work?</Chapter>

Most conversations about AI and jobs carry a hidden assumption. The amount of valuable work in the world is roughly fixed, so if machines do more of it, we need fewer people.

Suppose that over five years AI takes on enough tasks to free up the equivalent of 50 million full-time jobs.

In one future, employers turn those hours into payroll savings. Think of Britain's Industrial Revolution. Power looms spread, textile output surged, and handloom weavers' wages collapsed. The mill owner could look at the new machines and see a fortune. The weaver had to figure out how to feed a family on less. In the AI version, the people who own the technology pocket the gains while millions lose their jobs or scrape by on less.

In the other future, we get a renaissance of human flourishing that reaches people at every income level. Think of Florence. Painters figured out how to make a flat wall look like it opened onto another world, inspiring millions. Builders raised a brick dome too wide for the wooden supports of the day, changing architecture as we know it. Across Europe, printers figured out how to make hundreds of copies of a book without writing each one out by hand, putting books within reach of people who could never have afforded a manuscript. Astronomers worked out that Earth moves around the sun, changing where we thought we stood in the universe. Anatomists opened up human bodies and drew what they saw, giving doctors a more accurate picture of what they were trying to heal. In a few generations, people made as much progress as they had in the previous several centuries.

Imagine if work like that were part of millions of people's jobs. Use what AI saves us to give people the time, money, and room to take on problems we've lived with for generations. The work calls for creativity and gives people more say in what they do and a reason to care about it. Some will find ways to keep more children alive, build homes people can afford, or make clean energy cheaper. Others will build things we don't even know to ask for yet.

Now, you can't simply reassign millions of accountants and marketers to the work of preventing childhood deaths. We'll come back to what it takes to make that shift.

The work people get paid to do today is a small part of the valuable work humanity could be doing.

That gap is the opportunity, and it is enormous.

<Chapter number="Act IIIa">The problems we already know about</Chapter>

Parents turn down jobs because they can't find child care that covers their shift. Older adults who could live at home with a few hours of help a day have no reliable way to get it. Farmers lose part of their harvest between the field and the market. People leave prison ready to work and spend months looking for someone willing to hire them. Whole towns flood, rebuild, and flood again.

Nobody needs to discover these problems. Ask the people living with them. They can tell you exactly what a solution would be worth to them, which turns out to be a different question from what they can afford to pay for one.

The usual explanation is that the market doesn't reward solving these problems. If someone needs clean water and can't pay what it costs to deliver, no business serves them. The market reads that as insufficient demand.

We should be careful what we conclude from that.

<Turn>Capitalism is not a barometer of human morality.</Turn>

A problem without a profitable solution is still a problem people desperately want solved. Sometimes what's missing is an economic model that works at a price people can bear. Figuring out that model is itself a complex problem, and I'd like to see a lot more people get the chance to work on it.

I run a water company in Accra, Ghana. When the taps run dry, families buy water from trucks. Sending a truck just for one household can cost more than the family can pay.

But what if the truck was already going past their house?

That's what we're testing. An industrial customer buys enough water to make a route profitable. If we can deliver to households along the way, we can serve them at a lower cost. A family that used to call eight or nine drivers when the taps ran dry could count on a truck that already serves the neighborhood. The industrial orders could help pay for the next route. We have a long way to go before it scales. The breakthrough would be a business that grows by making water affordable to families we once couldn't afford to serve.

<Chapter number="Act IIIb">The problems we haven't imagined yet</Chapter>

<PostImage src="/posts/when-ai-does-our-work/weavers.webp" alt="A High Renaissance painting of an open-air workshop: two weavers in pale robes work a tall wooden loom beneath marble columns, while lengths of deep blue cloth are hoisted on ropes overhead and dancers in swirling red and white skirts move beneath them." />

Ask someone in 1926 what they'd want for a happy, comfortable life. They might tell you about a good home, a doctor nearby, enough food, meaningful work, time with family. Now imagine asking that same person what they'd want from a phone they could carry in their pocket. How much of your day would they have to imagine before it occurred to them to ask for turn-by-turn directions through a city they'd never visited, or a video call with a grandchild on another continent?

Once people can do something new, they start to discover what else they want. Somebody has to build the maps, make them useful on a street corner, and figure out how to tell you that the bridge ahead is closed before you've already turned onto it. Each thing we figure out gives somebody else a chance to see a problem worth solving.

We have problems we've known about for generations and barely touched. We'll have others that only become visible once we solve the ones in front of us. So why do we create so little value compared to what's possible? What would happen if a hundred times as many people had the training, the time, and the responsibility to figure some of this out? What about a thousand times as many?

<Chapter number="Act IV">What's actually stopping us?</Chapter>

If there are this many problems worth solving, why aren't we solving more of them?

Sometimes the answer is money. A clinic wants another dialysis machine and can't afford one. Sometimes the answer is technology. Suppose that same clinic gets a grant big enough to buy a machine for every patient who needs one. There will still be patients whose lives even the best dialysis machine we know how to build can't save.

The third answer is people. They can find ways to bring in money and develop technology that didn't exist before. In Accra, winning an industrial customer can give us the money to reach families we couldn't afford to serve before. The money becomes available because somebody figures out a way to earn it. The same is true of technology. Somewhere, people have to figure out why a treatment falls short, decide what to try, and stay with the problem long enough to discover something better.

That's why I keep coming back to how many people we teach and employ to solve complex problems. Each person who gets good at this work gives us another chance to solve a problem we've been stuck on. And sometimes what they figure out makes money or technology available to everyone who comes after them.

For thirty years, Denise's company needed her to close the books. As long as that took most of her time, the question of what else she might learn to do was largely beside the point. Then one Monday, AI takes on much of that work. The company still has clinics it can't reach. If it gives Denise time to find out why, she can start learning how to do something about it.

How many people whose time AI frees up will get to spend the next five years learning to solve problems like these? Who will help them learn? Who will pay for the work while the answer is still uncertain?

That's where the money companies save with AI should go.

<Chapter number="Act V">What if companies became portfolios of entrepreneurs?</Chapter>

Companies already pay people to work on problems they might never solve. They call it R&D. They fund several ideas knowing some will fail and a few might grow into whole businesses. As AI takes on more of the day-to-day work, companies can make those bets with people across the business.

<PostImage src="/posts/when-ai-does-our-work/drafting-hall.webp" alt="A High Renaissance oil painting of a vast workshop hall in warm afternoon light: a man studies a large technical drawing through a magnifying glass at a long table while a woman beside him looks on, others work at benches with instruments and papers behind them, a figure plays a viol near a sunlit doorway, and people watch from the wooden galleries above." />

A service technician knows which breakdown puts dialysis machines out of service for a week. Give her a few weeks, a small budget, and an engineer's time to see if she can keep the next one from breaking. Denise can take a different bet on the clinics that can't afford a machine. On her own, she might have to raise money just to test an idea. Her company already has engineers and machines on the bench. She could try something next week.

<Chapter number="Act VI">Entrepreneurs need coaching</Chapter>

A professional athlete can play at the highest level and still spend Monday reviewing film with a coach. An actor can work for thirty years and still rehearse a new scene with a director. People like Denise deserve that kind of coaching, too. The capacity to do this work is spread far more widely than the opportunity to learn it.

If I were Denise's manager, I'd give her three lost sales to look into. "Before we redesign the machine, let's find out what each clinic meant by 'can't afford it.' I'll make the first call with you."

The first clinic can cover the monthly payments. It's the deposit they can't afford. Denise could work out a payment plan. Before the next call, her manager asks how she'll find out what got in the way without bringing up the deposit. The second clinic has the money. What it can't afford is a week of downtime every time the machine needs a repair.

Does Denise call more clinics, talk to the service team, or try a payment plan? Her manager asks her to pick one and come back Friday with what she learned. It's her call. And if she comes back with a deal the clinic wants and the company can afford, somebody can actually say yes.

Set aside time for coaching every week. Replay five minutes of a call and listen for the question Denise almost asked. On the next call, she gets to ask it. A week looking into lost sales can lead to a pilot. As her judgment improves, she can take on bigger bets.

Suppose Denise tests the payment plan and finds it would leave the company short of cash to build the machines. She recommends ending the pilot. If her manager counts only sales, she has nothing to show for six weeks. If he sees the cost she spared the company, he might trust her with a bigger bet next time.

<Chapter number="Act VII">If people work like owners, they need to own something</Chapter>

Suppose one of Denise's bets works. She recruits a team and spends five years building a business worth $50 million. What should she earn? Her salary? A slightly bigger bonus? A promotion?

If Denise had raised money to start this business herself, she and her investors would have talked about who owned what before anyone wrote a check. That conversation belongs inside the company, too. Her employer put up the money and paid her through the experiments that failed. It deserves a return. So does Denise.

A technician who solves a costly repair problem might get a share of the savings. A team that opens a new market might share in what it earns. If Denise builds a venture that can stand on its own, founder-like equity should be on the table. Workers should help set those terms before anyone knows which idea will work.

A stake in the business could change when Denise can retire or how much she can help a kid pay for college. When she comes home and tells her family what she helped build, she ought to be able to tell them how much of it is theirs.

<Chapter number="Act VIII">Would I bet my own money on this?</Chapter>

Today, about fourteen public companies are worth a trillion dollars or more. My bet is that in ten years there'll be hundreds, and thousands more will bring in ten or a hundred times their current revenue. They'll get there by building things people would pay for today if anyone knew how to make them work. Some of the people who'll figure that out are closing the books in your company right now.

Let Denise go and you can tell the board, down to the dollar, how much less it'll cost to run the same business next year. Your competitors can make those cuts, too. The one I'd worry about is already paying people like Denise to figure out how to reach the customers you've never been able to serve.

A year later, Denise knows how to take a problem the company was stuck on, get the right people in a room, and stay with it until somebody can buy a machine they couldn't buy before. When the service technician comes to her with an idea about the breakdown that keeps putting machines out of service, Denise can pull up a chair and ask, "Want to make the first call together?"

That's the company I'd put my money on. A year ago it had an accountant it thought it could do without. Now it has a person working on problems it used to think it couldn't solve. Imagine what that company looks like in ten years. It will eat the companies that chose efficiency over capacity for breakfast.
