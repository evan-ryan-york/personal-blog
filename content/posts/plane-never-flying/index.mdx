<Lead>
There are times to iterate, and there are times to start over.
</Lead>

Almost every serious argument about American education assumes we are in the first situation. Smaller classes. Better curriculum. Higher pay. New standards, new assessments, new accountability. The whole conversation takes the basic shape of school as given and argues about how to tune it. Tweak the inputs, improve the outputs, iterate toward something that works.

<Highlight>
I no longer believe we are in that situation. I think we are in the second one.
</Highlight>

I keep coming back to one image. You would not blame the pilot for crashing a plane so riddled with design flaws it was never going to stay in the air. You would not study the pilot's technique, revise the pre-flight checklist, or add another gauge to the cockpit. You would look at the design, and you would start over. The people in the cockpit are doing extraordinary work under impossible conditions. But a plane that cannot stay in the air will crash, no matter who is flying it.

What we call "how school works" was never designed from what we know about how children actually learn. It is a hundred-year-old emergency response to chronic underfunding, built to turn a flood of immigrant children into a punctual factory workforce as cheaply as possible, and hardened over time into something we now mistake for the natural shape of education.

The arrangement where one adult holds thirty children at one pace through one sequence inside one box, while that same box is also asked to be clinic and cafeteria and counselor and rec center, is a failed design. It has never worked and it never will, and it should be abandoned. What children actually need, it was never shaped to give. And for the first time in a century, we can begin to see how to build its replacement. Not by putting children in front of screens all day, but by doing very nearly the opposite.

<Divider />

<Section number="01" label="The Design">
## The plane was never *built right*
</Section>

<Lead>
We know how children actually learn. We have known for a hundred years. The system reflects almost none of it.
</Lead>

None of what follows is contested in the field. It is not anecdote but decades of rigorous research, much of it replicated, argued over, and still standing after long peer review. It sits in every textbook and on every school-of-education wall, and we built the country's children a system that ignores all of it.

**Children build their own understanding.** No one can pour it into them. Vygotsky called it the zone of proximal development: learning happens at the narrow edge between what a child can already do and what they can do with help, and that edge sits in a different place for every child in the room.<FootnoteRef id={1} /> The century of work that followed, from Dewey to Papert, made the same point in different language. Understanding is constructed, not delivered. A child doesn't grasp that one-quarter is half of a half because a teacher says so. It clicks when a few things line up at once: they already understand what it means to split something in two, they can stretch that idea to splitting it into four parts that have to be equal, and they meet it in a form that actually means something to them — quarters of a football game, four pieces of a candy bar, the marks on a measuring cup. Get the context wrong and nothing lands. Get it right, with the groundwork already there, and the idea arrives on its own. But that moment doesn't come for every child on the same day. A lesson aimed at the middle of the room is aimed at no one who is actually in it. **Half the class is lost and the other half is bored, and we average the two and call it grade level.**

<PostImage src="/posts/plane-never-flying/classroom.png" alt="A packed classroom of diverse elementary school children at desks" caption="Thirty children, one pace, one box — the design we mistake for the natural shape of school." />

On the NAEP test, the one standardized assessment American students have been taking since 1970, scores at the end of high school have barely moved in the entire half-century since. Seventeen-year-olds, the finished product of thirteen years of schooling, read and do math at roughly their 1970s level. There were slight gains from the early 1990s into the early 2010s, and then they eroded — a slide that began before the pandemic and that COVID turned into a cliff.<FootnoteRef id={2} /> And still we hand every teacher the same lessons, in the same order, for every child, year after year, and profess surprise when nothing changes.

**A child learns inside relationships, or not at all.** This is not soft, and it is not optional. Zaretta Hammond has shown that the brain will not cross into deep, effortful thinking unless it feels safe and connected first.<FootnoteRef id={3} /> Trust is a precondition for cognition, not a courtesy laid on top of it. But trust is not generic warmth. A child feels safe with an adult who actually knows them, and they feel known when the learning starts from who they already are: their language, their neighborhood, the knowledge their family and community already carry. The opposite of that is a classroom that treats a child's whole identity as a deficit to be corrected, and Lisa Delpit documented in painful detail who absorbs that harm first.<FootnoteRef id={4} /> Poor children and children of color, handed a curriculum built as though their lives were a problem, are told in a hundred quiet ways that the person they are is the thing getting in the way of learning. No one thinks well from inside that message.

**The most powerful thing we have ever measured, we have never been able to give every child.** Put the edge and the relationship together. When an adult a child trusts meets them exactly where they are and helps them take the next step, the gains are enormous, and no form of group instruction has ever been proven to come close. Benjamin Bloom set the field that exact challenge in 1984: teach thirty children at once as well as a single tutor teaches one.<FootnoteRef id={5} /> Forty years later, no one has done it. Not for any reason that has to do with pedagogy. For a reason that has to do with arithmetic. Quality individual attention works, and it has always been too expensive to give to every child.

**A young child's most serious work is play.** Play is not the reward a child earns once the real work is done. For a young child, play is the real work. Peter Gray has assembled the evidence that free, child-directed play is the engine through which young humans build judgment, resilience, social fluency, and the capacity to govern themselves, the same way every other young mammal rehearses its way into adulthood.<FootnoteRef id={6} /> At that age the work is inseparable from the body. Running, climbing, building, learning by trial what is safe and what is not. Children are embodied, outdoor animals, and their first learning is physical long before it is abstract.

We built a model that keeps them seated, indoors, and still for most of their waking childhood. And when their bodies do what bodies are built to do, fidget, squirm, refuse to sit one more minute, too often we hand them a screen to quiet the impulse, until they go still and compliant in a plane that was never built to fly. Over the same half-century we spent stripping childhood of free play and filling it with adult-directed instruction, anxiety and depression among the young have risen sharply.<FootnoteRef id={7} /> Researchers argue hard about how much of that to lay at the feet of lost play versus social media versus everything else, but the line moves the wrong way no matter how you cut it. We did not remove something pleasant. **We removed a developmental necessity and renamed the vacancy rigor.**

<PostImage src="/posts/plane-never-flying/play.png" alt="A large group of diverse young children playing outdoors" caption="Play is not the reward once the real work is done. For a young child, it is the real work." />

**As a child grows, the work grows up with them.** The drive that comes alive in free play does not fade with age. It matures. A teenager is no longer rehearsing the world through tag and make-believe. They are pulled toward the things that actually move human lives: justice, power, belonging, love, the urge to make something other people will use. But that's too messy. It can't be measured. It can't be done by everyone at once, at the same pace, in the same way. A factory simply can't let each thing it produces have agency over what it becomes. So we ask them to trade their ambition for ambivalence. Their curiosity for compliance. We tell them to hit pause on everything that feels natural about learning, and to trust us that it will pay off one day. Seymour Papert showed that we learn most deeply while building something real and external, something we care about and can hand to another person.<FootnoteRef id={8} /> Dewey said it a century earlier: we learn by doing, in work that carries genuine consequences.<FootnoteRef id={9} /> Give a child ownership of work that matters and motivation stops being something you have to manufacture.

This isn't "making the word problem relevant by mentioning basketball." It's getting out of the classroom to start a food pantry. It's fixing the roof on the shed that stores the gym equipment. It's making art, growing and cooking your own food, going down to the courthouse to fight the rent hike pushing your friends out of their homes. **Authenticity is not a mask you wrap around your curriculum.** Authenticity is the curriculum, and you can't manufacture it, mass-produce it, or standardize it.

**A child is not raw material, and learning is not something we install in them.** This is the mistake beneath all the others. Frank Smith showed that human beings learn constantly, effortlessly, without noticing, the way they learn to talk, to read a face, to belong to the people they identify with.<FootnoteRef id={10} /> School runs on the opposite assumption: that learning is occasional and effortful, that it happens only when an adult delivers it and a test confirms it. Paulo Freire followed the line to its end.<FootnoteRef id={11} /> If a child is not an empty vessel but a living, learning mind, then no education is ever neutral. Every classroom is either enlarging that mind's power to read the world and act on it, or training it to sit down and take the world as given.

Put those together and the conclusion is darker than any one of them. A machine built to fill empty vessels, aimed at a child who was never empty, cannot fill them. So it does the only thing it can. It teaches them to stop offering what they have. The child who "fails" to learn long division has not stopped learning. They have learned something else, and learned it perfectly: that they are not a math person, that school is not for them, that the safest thing to do with their own mind is to keep it to themselves.

<Highlight>
We do not manufacture children who cannot learn. We manufacture children who have learned to distrust their own minds, and to wait for someone else to tell them what they are worth.
</Highlight>

These are not competing theories. They are one design:

<Blueprint>
<BlueprintItem number="01" title="Meet each child where they actually are.">
There is no class all ready for the same thing on the same day, and there never was. Let each child move through what they are ready for, the day they are ready for it.
</BlueprintItem>
<BlueprintItem number="02" title="Build the relationship and safety that thinking requires." />
<BlueprintItem number="03" title="Begin from who a child already is.">
Their interests, their culture, the knowledge their family and community carry. Treat that as ground to build on, not a deficit to fix.
</BlueprintItem>
<BlueprintItem number="04" title="Protect their play." />
<BlueprintItem number="05" title="Give them real work, with real stakes, that they own." />
<BlueprintItem number="06" title="Get them outside." />
<BlueprintItem number="07" title="Aim the whole thing at agency, not compliance." />
<BlueprintItem number="08" title="Wrap all of it in the individual attention that makes every other piece possible." />
</Blueprint>

That is not a wishlist. It is the blueprint. We have had it for a century, and built children the opposite anyway. So how did we get here?

<Section number="02" label="The History">
## A century of *triage*
</Section>

<Lead>
Because it was never built to do anything else. It was built to be cheap.
</Lead>

We did not choose the factory model because we believed it would help children learn. We backed into it at the turn of the twentieth century, because it was the cheapest way to take millions of immigrant children pouring into industrializing cities and turn them, fast and at scale, into the punctual, orderly workforce that economy wanted. We don't have to infer this. The men who built the system wrote it down. Ellwood Cubberley, the first dean of Stanford's school of education, whose textbooks trained a generation of American superintendents, described the task without flinching:<FootnoteRef id={12} />

<Callout>
"Our schools are, in a sense, factories in which the raw products (children) are to be shaped and fashioned into products to meet the various demands of life."
</Callout>

<PostImage src="/posts/plane-never-flying/factory.png" alt="A 1900s factory floor with workers, including children, at the machines" caption="The raw products, shaped and fashioned to meet the various demands of life." />

That is the base the whole enterprise was built on, and we have been trying to season our way out of it ever since. It is a stew started from a cheap, spoiled cut of meat. Every few years we throw in another spice, a new standard, a new test, a new accountability regime, a new app, hoping to fix the flavor without touching the rot at the bottom of the pot. It never works, because the problem was never the seasoning.

And the underfunding is worse than it looks, because the mandate keeps growing. We ask one institution, funded at maybe a tenth of what the job would honestly cost, to be school and clinic and cafeteria and counselor and rec center and trauma ward at once, and then act surprised when it does none of them well. The failure is not the people's. It is arithmetic. Nearly every school in the country runs in permanent triage, reaching the few fires it can while the rest burn. The pilots are extraordinary. The plane cannot fly.

And money alone was never the cure, because spent inside this design it only buys more of the design: a teacher for fifteen children instead of thirty, still a class, still one pace. So the system answers its own failure the only way it can, with more of what failed. More remediation, more seat time, more test prep, every hour of it taken from somewhere else, the art class, the recess, the afternoon that should have gone to building something or simply being a child. That is the hidden tax, and the flat scores are the least of it. The deeper loss, the attention, the appetite for a hard book, the fluency that grows only in unstructured hours, is the kind no test was ever built to count.

So why haven't we changed it?

<Section number="03" label="The Trap">
## *Resignation*, not conviction
</Section>

I have spent my whole career in education, as a teacher, a principal, a chief academic officer, a founder. The facts above are well known in all the systems I've worked in. The system's sharpest critics are not outsiders throwing rocks. They are the people inside it.

<PostImage src="/posts/plane-never-flying/ladder.png" alt="A single impossibly tall ladder rising into empty sky" caption="Climb to the top, and there is no one there who chose this — the ladder was leaning against nothing." />

Not because the people inside are foolish or cruel. Because almost none of them believe they have the power to change it.

Ask a teacher why they still run a scope and sequence they know won't reach most of the kids in front of them, and they'll tell you the principal requires it. Ask the principal, and it's the district. Ask the district, and it's the school board. Ask the board, and it's the lawmakers. Ask the lawmakers, and it's the budget, the mandate, the last election. Everyone is carrying out an order from someone one rung up. And when you finally climb to the top of the ladder, there is no one there who chose this.

<Highlight>
This is the quiet engine that keeps a broken model running: not conviction, but resignation. A vast institution run by people, almost none of whom would design it this way, each certain that the authority to change it lives one rung up, until the ladder runs out and you find it was leaning against nothing.
</Highlight>

If no one inside the system will break the cycle, the obvious hope is that reform will force it from outside. It hasn't, because reform has never aimed at the shape. Every time the federal government has acted on education in my lifetime, it reached for the same lever and turned it harder: Clinton's national standards, Bush's testing and sanctions, Obama's waivers and evaluations.<FootnoteRef id={13} /> Different parties, one instinct, never once asking whether the plane could fly, only bolting another gauge to the cockpit.

And the money to do more is not coming. Not from the right, which has decided public education is a front in the culture war rather than a public good. Not from the left, which takes schools for granted between elections and, when it finally acts, reinforces the very cage it claims to want to open. No president of my lifetime, of either party, has changed the shape of the thing, and I don't expect the next one to, whoever wins. That is not a reason for despair. It is a reason to stop waiting. If the redesign is going to happen, it will not be handed down. It will be built.

<Section number="04" label="The Redesign">
## The *redesign*: reclaiming the money and the time
</Section>

<Lead>
Picture a neighborhood a few years from now that decided to stop waiting.
</Lead>

The school building is still standing, but walk inside and it is unrecognizable. The corridor of identical classrooms is gone. In its place, several different organizations have set up shop, renting the rooms cheaply and drawing on a pool of money that now funds the experiences each child needs, weighted hardest toward the children who have the least. What those organizations are depends entirely on where you're standing. In rural Colorado, one runs the mountains: camping, fishing, watershed science you do with your boots in the creek. In downtown Atlanta, the old gym is a dance company that doubles as a recording studio and a real small business. On the coast it's boatbuilding. In a farm town, a working farm. In a city, a theater, a bike co-op, a coding guild, a community kitchen. None of it is franchised. None of it was handed down from a state office. Each place grew the experiences its own people already knew how to give.

<PostImage src="/posts/plane-never-flying/watershed.png" alt="Children doing watershed science in a mountain creek, boots in the water" caption="Watershed science you do with your boots in the creek — the experiences a place already knows how to give." />

And the experiences answer what a place is actually carrying. A neighborhood living with a lot of childhood trauma builds out real therapy and mentoring. A neighborhood fighting obesity builds community gardens and brings in people who teach kids to grow and cook their own food. A neighborhood hollowed out by opioids and alcohol builds social services, after-school programs that run late, and internships in the clinics trying to hold the line.

<Highlight>
This is the part the old system could never imagine: that a town might be funded to build its children's education out of itself.
</Highlight>

<Scene label="A day, a few years from now">

A ten-year-old, call her Maya, gets dropped off at her school building. She does not go inside.

She climbs into the green van with six kayaks strapped to the roof, and it pulls out toward the lake.

Her crew is ten kids and two adults who have had them since the fall. Last week, rangers at Lake Arkabutla, the flood-control reservoir an hour south of Memphis, taught them to paddle and to read the water.

The water is low this year. The drought has done something strange: small islands have surfaced in the middle of the lake, and things have started living on them.

The county wants to raise the water again. Maya's crew is documenting what is out there first, so that someone actually knows what the flood would drown.

So this morning they paddle out to one of the islands.

Maya photographs a beetle she can't name. She finds a line of tracks, maybe a squirrel, and cannot for the life of her work out how a squirrel got to the middle of a lake. She adds two new birds to the crew's list.

They kayak back, load the van, and drive to a college lab nearby, where a grad student shows them how to test the soil they bagged.

Back at the building, they upload the day and run the photos through an AI that helps them put names to what they found.

Then the hard part. The adults put a question on the board: is it right to manage a river this way, knowing that each time the water rises, whatever has started to live on those islands dies?

Maya has to pick a side and defend it. She spent the morning on that island. It is not an abstract question to her anymore.

Lunch is several food trucks pulled up outside, run by restaurants from down the road. Maya and a handful of others work the line, prepping and cooking what the building eats before they sit down to eat it.

After that comes the quiet part of the day. Maya works through fractions with a math tutor that is patient in a way no adult with thirty children could be. It knows exactly what she has and hasn't gotten, keeps her at the edge where the work is hard but not yet impossible, and ties the mathematical ideas back to the island, the distances she paddled, the samples she counted. They talk it through. It is cheap enough now to give to every child, which it never was before.

Then the gym, which becomes a music hall in the afternoon. Maya plays cello in the youth ensemble. They are working a Haydn piece and she is still learning to read the score, so she starts with a tutor that watches through a camera, fixes her bow hold, and walks her through a little theory. Then she plays it with the others, which is the part that matters.

Late in the day she takes her book to the spot she likes, between two trees at the edge of campus. She reads about insects, because the island got under her skin. After a while a literacy tutor asks her what she read and lets her tell it, then spends ten minutes on metaphor, drawing its examples from writers on music, because it knows that is the way in to her.

She finishes with her soccer team, on her soccer pitch. She is learning the game, but her coach cares more about the thing the team keeps failing at, which is playing like a team at all.

</Scene>

Say it plainly: there was AI all through Maya's day. The fractions, the bow hold, the metaphor lesson, the species on the island, each had a machine somewhere underneath. And at no point was a screen the thing she was actually doing. The machine did the parts a machine does fastest, in a fraction of the time they used to eat, and the money and the hours that once vanished into the billion-dollar test-prep machine went instead into the lake, the ensemble, the debate, the team, into everything a century of research says actually grows a mind. That is the whole trade. It is the bow hold but never the music, the fractions but never the debate, the mechanics of a thing but never the meaning of it. AI plugs in where it does the most good, then gets out of the way, and what it gets out of the way for is a childhood.

<Section number="05" label="The Choice">
## *Liberation*, not indoctrination
</Section>

Step back far enough and you notice that none of this is actually new. It only looks futuristic because the thing it replaces has been around so long we forgot it was an invention.

For nearly all of human history, children did not learn in age-sorted rows inside a purpose-built box, cut off from the life of the community around them. They learned embedded in that life: mentored, apprenticed, watched over by a web of adults who knew them, in mixed-age groups, doing real work that mattered to people they could see.<FootnoteRef id={14} /> The isolated classroom is not the timeless form of education. It is the recent aberration, barely older than the automobile, and we've mistaken its familiarity for permanence.

The village had exactly one thing it could never guarantee: that every child, regardless of which adults happened to surround them, would receive patient, expert, individual instruction. That was always the privilege of the wealthy. The tutor was the dividing line, the thing the rich could buy and the poor could not, generation after generation, which is a great deal of how the rich stayed rich. For the first time in the history of our species, we can imagine handing that one gift to every child at once. The single resource that always divided children could become the one that equalizes them.

<PostImage src="/posts/plane-never-flying/community.png" alt="A mixed-age community of children and adults learning together" caption="Mentored, apprenticed, watched over by a web of adults who knew them — the way children learned for nearly all of human history." />

<Highlight>
Do not mistake my optimism for naiveté.
</Highlight>

The same technology that could free the time and money to raise whole people could just as easily pipe a flat, surveilled, engagement-optimized curriculum into millions of passive children at industrial scale. Indoctrination, finally perfected. The naive bet is that the good version arrives on its own. It will not. The dark version is the default: it has the business model, and it is the one being built right now, by people optimizing for time-on-screen and calling it learning. The better one has to be built on purpose, against that current, by people who want something else for children. And two of them are worth a direct word.

**To the builders:** this part is mostly yours, and I mean it as an invitation. AI is a raw material. The tools and products that would build the future above do not exist yet, which means you decide what they become. Build each one with liberation as the design spec. Optimize for the child who outgrows it, not the one who can't put it down. The best version is the one that disappears into a childhood instead of eating it. So build that one.

**To the educators, my own people:** you've known all of this the whole time. You're the pilots who spent your careers flying the broken plane and getting blamed for the crashes. I understand the skepticism. You were promised that Blended Learning, then Personalized Learning, then Gamified Learning, then Adaptive Learning would each be the magic wand, and here we are, arguably further from what school should be than we have ever been.

This article is not a promise. The future I'm hoping for is not a guarantee, and given the incentives in play right now, it is actually unlikely. But here is why I'm writing it anyway: AI is different from everything on that list. The genie is out of the bottle, and it is not going back in. It will reach every school and every classroom no matter what any of us think of it. You cannot vote it away or wait it out. The only choice left is whether teachers help shape it, or leave it to people who have never set foot in a classroom. Harnessed right, it does not replace you. It creates space for more joy, more curiosity, more human connection, more love and liberation.

Will it be free? No. We will have to learn, as a country, how to fund this new raising of children. Will it be easy? No. It will take a whole new body of policy, and that policy has one job before any other: to make these experiences actually work for the children the old system fails worst, the students with disabilities, the ones carrying trauma, the ones with behavioral needs, the ones still learning the language. The money stays public and runs hardest toward the children with the least, or all we have done is rebuild the old injustice with shinier tools.

This will be challenging. But my god, challenge is the only thing education has been for fifty years, and we have been losing the whole time. So dream. Force something better. There is a new power loose in the world, and for once it has not yet been gobbled up and monopolized and aimed at someone's bottom line. Point it at the most ambitious thing we can imagine for a child.

<Callout>
Metal can become a gun or a trumpet. Wood can become a spear or a home. AI is not the enemy; it is the raw material of the century, and what gets made of it is up to us. The world has enough cynics. What it has always been short on is the dreamers.
</Callout>
